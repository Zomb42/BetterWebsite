# Self-destructive Altruism

I feel like often times in life we come upon some profound “moments”, often emotionally charged, that for a 
second break us free from our consciousness to revel in that experience. Afterwards though, we usually leave it be, 
perhaps dedicating a page to it in our story, but not carrying it with us as we journey onwards. We’ll revisit it 
from time to time, as we get reminded of its essence through something similar, or perhaps completely randomly as 
we lay in bed one night and let our thoughts drift. I feel like that’s how one normally processes these experiences, 
and probably the best and healthiest way, but every once in a while, a single line refuses to be left alone.

For me, it was this one:

“We’re lonely and sad and empty and self-destructive, and so we turned to a kind of conceited, self-satisfied altruism.”

...

Three Days of Happiness by Sugaru Miaki has to be one of my favorite light novels, with the way it explores the world 
of meaning in our lives and the way our perceptions of it change depending on our situation and relationship with others. 
The main male and female protagonists are deeply unhappy with their lives, which interweave a deeply mundane tragedy, 
and by the end, the misfortune seems every bit as heavy, but their lives have become a bit more palatable. 
It’s bittersweet in that such despair can only be resolved to a point, but I feel like this melancholic reflection 
on the ordinary is incredibly beautiful compared to the other media that is often consumed. Yes, we should strive 
to be heroes of ourselves, but real life is often marred with this pensive glitter that makes our existence sparkle a bit more.
While the story primarily focuses on the idea of value and meaning and its relationship with time, I feel perhaps 
its most jarring line has to be the one above. Looking at it, the paradox is incredibly obvious. Altruism is an 
act dedicated to the concern of others, whose motivation is by definition devoid of personal gain, so anything done 
in a self-centered way shouldn’t even be regarded as altruism. Grammatically, “conceited, self-satisfied altruism” 
shouldn’t exist. It also appears more glaring than a normal oxymoron, as it isn’t just that the definitions are 
different, but that one depends on the negation of the other.

Its existence shouldn’t have meaning, mirroring the lives of the main characters, but just like how the characters 
in some essence were able to create meaning, so too I believe there can be significant meaning found in this statement. 
When someone acts conceited and self-satisfied, one could imagine they would pursue very selfish acts, like stealing 
and placing their own priority above others, but I think this guess kind of ignores the first half of the thought.

“We’re lonely and sad and empty and self-destructive”

Driven to the point of overwhelming dread (more greatly emphasized with the repeated adjectives and the three ands), 
the characters still recognize goodness as a desirable thing to bring forth, even though they aren’t able to experience it themselves. 
In this way, their altruism seems almost like a way to establish control in a life outside of their will, and at the same 
time a desperate projection of their wish upon others. They try to help others in such a self-destructive manner, 
as they aren’t able to help themselves, yet still “selfishly” wish to be saved as well.

What stood out to me on a second look is that nobody in the story ever calls them conceited except for themselves. 
From the outside, what they do is simply kind, and the people they help only ever feel the help. The conceit lives 
entirely in how the characters see themselves, as if they distrust their own goodness so much that they can’t even 
let it be called altruism, and have to call it vanity instead. I suppose that’s a quiet way of being self-destructive, 
to not be able to keep even the good you’ve done.

The characters help each other and technically “love” each other on the outside, but it’s driven by this desire 
to validate their own life with meaning, to hear from someone else that it counts for something. I don’t think 
that makes the love any less real. It just points to the fact that this tragedy is found within genuinely good 
people, only covered with this brokenness. By the end of the novel, such tragedy between the main characters is 
made ever more sweet and ever more bitter, which develops the main theme of the futility of time, but what stood 
out to me is the idea of the tragic ordinary, and how this story invites us to consider it.

Perhaps we are lucky to not notice it, but I feel like ordinary desperation often seeps into our lives as we tackle 
the ebbs and flows of life ahead. It might not be worth a page, but its core is what gives our stories their binding, 
as it’s how we try to fit our needs with what we can do.
