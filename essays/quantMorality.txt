# The Moral Case for Quantitative Finance

Forgive me for the length of this piece. I think the topic deserves a real ground up treatment rather than a hot take, so I'd rather build the argument carefully than rush it.

People throw around the idea that quantitative finance is a career that exists purely to make money and offers nothing back to society. I think this view is reinforced by two things. First, there simply aren't many quants, so almost nobody outside the industry actually knows what the job involves. Second, the handful of quants who are loud about their work online often lean into the villain image themselves, whether as a joke, a flex, or because they genuinely see it that way. Combine that with the fact that quants have no real incentive to explain themselves publicly, and you get a vacuum that gets filled by whatever story is easiest to believe. I'd also guess some of this perception comes from a more basic human impulse: it feels good to have a villain. Pointing at a small group of quiet, extremely well paid people who don't appear to make anything tangible is a convenient way to feel morally justified without much risk of pushback.

I want to push back on that pushback. I came into this with the same instinct, that this industry was basically legalized greed, and a summer inside it changed my mind enough that I keep coming back to defend it. This is an opinion piece, not an attempt to score careers on some objective morality index. If there's one exception, it's taxes: quants pay a lot of them, and taxes paid is about as close to an objective, society-endorsed measure of contribution as we have. But that's a small piece of the argument, not the whole thing.

## What Do We Mean by Morality, and What Does Society Expect of a Career?

Any conversation about whether a career is moral needs a working definition of morality first. There are countless definitions rooted in different religious and philosophical traditions, but a serviceable general one is that morality is a system of principles and standards that help people distinguish right from wrong. Everyone's version of that system differs somewhat, but I think most people agree on a common core: helping someone in genuine need is good, and hurting an innocent person for your own benefit is bad. I'll skip the harder questions of what counts as "need" or "innocent," since that's not the point here. The point is that a shared baseline exists, and it's a good thing that it exists, because it lets us collectively reward good behavior and discourage bad behavior.

That baseline morality naturally extends to how we view careers. We should want people to pursue relatively moral work, and society reflects that: being a doctor is respected and well compensated, while being a violent criminal is outlawed. At the same time, we understand that some people take less moral paths, like petty theft or sex work, out of desperation rather than preference, and we tend to extend more empathy to them than to someone in the same position by choice.

It's worth pausing on the doctor example, because even a career society treats as unambiguously moral isn't moral unconditionally. A doctor does something good by healing people inside a system of rules: informed consent, safety standards, licensing. The moment a doctor starts selling organs on the black market or refuses emergency treatment to extract payment, the exact same medical skill becomes something we'd readily call immoral, even criminal. The skill didn't change. The law-bound context around it did. I'm raising this early because it ends up being the hinge for the entire rest of this argument: quantitative finance follows the same pattern. The tools themselves are morally neutral, and it's the rules governing how they're used that decide whether the outcome is beneficial or predatory.

This suggests society applies a kind of sliding moral expectation based on someone's circumstances. Someone in a difficult situation is held to a lower moral standard than someone born into privilege, and the existence of social welfare and progressive taxation reflects that intuition: those with more are expected to give more. But I don't think this expectation should scale directly with income or job title, the way the tax code does. A fair tax system taxes two people earning the same income in the same city the same amount, regardless of how they got there, and reserves other forms of privilege (say, inherited property) for separate taxation. I don't think morality works the same way.

### A Thought Experiment

Take two software engineers at the same company. Person A earns $200k a year and grew up in poverty with an unstable home life, clawing their way into this position against real odds. Person B earns $150k a year, grew up wealthy, attended elite schools, had connections that opened doors to running a nonprofit or taking a much higher paying offer elsewhere, and chose this job specifically for the work life balance.

From a tax standpoint, it's fair that Person A pays more, since they earn more. But from a moral standpoint, I'd argue Person B actually carries the larger unmet obligation. They had more paths available to them and picked comfort over impact, which I'd call a quietly self-interested choice even though it looks modest on paper. My point here isn't to assign everyone a fixed moral quota based on their background. It's the opposite: it shows how hard it actually is to pin a specific moral requirement on someone from the outside, and that chasing more compensation isn't automatically the immoral choice in the scenario. Person A optimizing for money after a hard start is not obviously less moral than Person B optimizing for comfort after an easy one.

## Should Talent Come With a Moral Obligation?

That thought experiment actually previews the argument I want to make next, just from a different angle. If Person B's choice of comfort over more compensation earns no public scrutiny, but a quant's choice of compensation over "more meaningful" work earns plenty, that gap is telling. It suggests the complaint was never really about squandered talent in the first place. It's about the money. Let me make the same underlying point through a more direct, rights-based argument instead.

A common argument against quant finance is that these are clearly bright people who should be doing something more "productive," like agricultural research or fixing government systems. I think this argument has a real flaw: it assumes that certain traits should obligate someone to a certain kind of work.

In a healthy market economy, we generally want the most skilled people in a category to end up doing the work that needs that skill, and free labor allocation is what makes that possible. But that only works if people are actually free to allocate themselves, meaning they aren't boxed in by expectations tied to their talent. A skilled lumberjack should weigh the value of chopping wood against how much they actually want to do it, and if they'd rather do something else, they should be free to leave without being shamed for "wasting" their skill. The moment we start assigning people to roles based on what they're good at rather than what they choose, we're one step from some genuinely ugly history, like justifications for forced labor along racial lines. Maybe more societal value would come from a quant working in agriculture in some abstract sense, but the market has priced their skill differently, and it isn't anyone's place to compel them otherwise.

I want to be careful here, because freedom to choose a career can't be the whole justification on its own, or it would just as easily defend choosing to be an arms dealer or a scammer. Freedom explains why nobody should be able to order a talented person into a specific job. It doesn't by itself say the job they do choose is a good one. That part of the argument comes back to the law-bound point I opened with: a career built on skill is fine to choose freely, and whether it's moral depends on whether the actual work, done within the rules that govern it, helps people or preys on them. The freedom argument answers "why shouldn't quants be told what to do with their talent." The rest of this essay is trying to answer the separate question of whether what they actually do with it is good. And if we go back to the luck and circumstance argument, plenty of people more privileged and more talented than the average quant are doing far more ordinary work, simply because that's what they chose.

## Disassociating Morality From Income

Put together, this means we shouldn't judge the morality of a career purely by how much it pays or how it's perceived. There's an instinct, especially around finance, that because a career is lucrative and a little opaque, it must be extracting value rather than creating it. I don't think that holds up. What actually determines whether a career is moral is the real world impact of the work being done, which, once you look closely at quantitative finance, makes a pretty strong case that it's a societally beneficial career when practiced within the bounds of the law.

## What Quants Actually Do

Before judging the impact, it's worth being honest about how little most people understand this job. High income careers like engineering, medicine, and law come with a fairly intuitive story: you can point to the bridge, the surgery, the contract. Traditional finance, like investment banking, is less intuitive but still legible, since people can see that a merger or acquisition results in a more efficient company. Quantitative trading doesn't have that visible output. There's no product, no client meeting, and by most rough estimates the number of people who do this for a living worldwide is small, likely well under a hundred thousand. So the public image fills in with whatever's easiest: quants "play with the economy," which collapses into two unflattering stories. One treats them like glorified day traders, closer to gamblers extracting money from a zero sum game than professionals. The other, more structural version treats markets as a pool of value that quants skim off the top without creating anything, which echoes a broader populist narrative, one that shows up in different forms across the political spectrum, that the wealthy generally profit by extracting value that labor actually created rather than creating any of their own.

So what does the job actually involve? Broadly, "quantitative" just means using statistics and data to make decisions, which technically describes a huge number of careers. To keep this focused, I'm talking specifically about quants who trade in public markets through proprietary trading firms. Their work mostly comes down to two closely related functions: price discovery and market making.

### Price Discovery

Price discovery is the process by which the collective decisions of market participants converge on the true value of an asset. It's basic supply and demand in action: if something is undervalued, buying pressure (including from quants) pushes the price up, and if it's overvalued, selling pressure pushes it down.

Why does this matter? Because a capitalist economy needs accurate prices to allocate resources well. Someone has to signal how much wood, corn, or semiconductor capacity the economy actually needs, and in a market system, that signal comes from price rather than from central planning. Compare this to an economy with heavier central planning, where the government makes a larger share of these allocation calls directly rather than leaving them to price signals. In a market system, if wood is properly priced, that price tells producers and workers whether more wood should be produced or whether their labor would be more valuable somewhere else. Prices near equilibrium are what let the whole system approach maximum societal utility. Quants pushing prices toward their true value aren't skimming off that process, they're the mechanism that makes the process work at all.

### Market Making and Liquidity

The second function, market making, is where quants commit to buying or selling a financial asset at a small edge over its theoretical fair value, essentially agreeing to always be a counterparty for a small spread. On the surface this can look parasitic, since the market maker isn't the one who created the underlying asset. I thought this myself before I understood the mechanics. But providing this service isn't compulsory for anyone else, and it doesn't harm the people who choose not to use it; it simply gives everyone else an additional option to trade when they want to.

That option turns out to matter a lot. Market making does three concrete things for the economy:

**It stabilizes the market.** When there's always a willing buyer and seller at a reasonable price, individual trades don't send prices lurching around, which keeps the system predictable enough for people to actually plan around it.

**It prevents desperation selling and buying.** Without reliable market makers, someone who suddenly needs to sell an asset (to raise cash in an emergency, say) might have to accept a terrible price simply because no buyer happens to be around at that moment. Market makers absorb that timing mismatch so people aren't punished purely for bad timing.

**It creates more options, and competition keeps those options fair.** Because multiple market makers compete for the same order flow, the "edge" they charge gets squeezed down toward something closer to the actual cost of providing the service, rather than settling on whatever a single monopolist would charge. That competitive tightening is what lets the option genuinely serve the person trading rather than exploit them.

Underneath all three of these is a basic trust argument: people only participate in an economy, whether by buying stocks or investing savings, if they trust that its prices are stable and its markets are liquid enough to get in and out. Market making is a big part of what earns that trust.

### The Role of High-Frequency Trading

A lot of the public suspicion toward quant finance is really suspicion toward high-frequency trading (HFT) specifically, so it's worth separating the two, and worth being precise about what I mean by HFT here. I'm not talking about prop trading in general, which mostly operates on timescales of seconds to days. I mean the sub-second infrastructure layer underneath it: firms building custom FPGAs, laying private fiber and microwave links, and shaving nanoseconds off the time it takes a price update to travel from an exchange to a trading engine and back.

No individual customer notices a nanosecond. If you place a market order for a stock, you cannot perceive whether it executed 200 nanoseconds or 800 nanoseconds after you hit submit. So at the individual order level, this entire arms race can look like pure waste, engineering effort spent on a margin nobody experiences. But the value isn't at the individual order level, it's at the aggregate level. Faster infrastructure shrinks the gap between "new information exists" and "the market price reflects it" across every price update happening across every asset, all day, every day. Multiply an imperceptible improvement by the sheer volume of a modern market and it stops being imperceptible. It's the difference between a market that reflects reality with a small, constant lag and one that reflects it almost immediately. That's a direct extension of the price discovery argument I made earlier, just pushed down to a timescale most people never think about.

There's a real caveat here though, which is that speed can also curdle into something bad if it's allowed to concentrate rather than compete. If a single firm permanently outpaces everyone else's infrastructure, they stop being one of several competing market makers keeping each other honest, and start being the only one who can reliably see and react to new information first. At that point, the benefit of tighter spreads and faster price discovery can turn into something closer to a toll booth, where that firm extracts a small edge on nearly every trade simply because nobody else can compete on speed. This is why exchanges and regulators care about things like fair colocation access, and why some markets have deliberately introduced tiny "speed bumps" that cap how much advantage raw speed alone can buy. A monopoly on speed is bad for the same reason any monopoly is bad: competition is what keeps the edge a market maker charges close to the actual cost of providing the service, and a speed monopoly removes that pressure entirely. This is really the same law-bound point from earlier applied at a technical level: the industry stays moral only as long as nobody is allowed to win the speed race so completely that competition stops functioning.

Separate from the monopoly issue, the criticism with more teeth is around events like flash crashes, where HFT firms pull their quotes en masse during extreme volatility and leave the market thinner exactly when it's needed most. That's a legitimate design flaw worth regulating around, but it's a critique of specific risk controls and market structure rules, not evidence that sub-second speed itself is illegitimate.

I'll also float a looser analogy here, since it's been on my mind even if I can't fully defend it with rigor: think of the market as a system constantly settling toward equilibrium after every new piece of information, the way a physical system settles toward a lower energy state after a disturbance. Each price move triggers a cascade of reactions from other participants, who react to each other, and so on, until the system settles. Sub-second infrastructure accelerates that settling process at the smallest possible timescale. If reaching equilibrium quickly is good for the reasons I laid out above, then whatever accelerates that process, even if it looks chaotic and imperceptible up close, is doing something useful in aggregate.

## Where the Line Actually Is: Rules, Public Markets, and Private Markets

None of this is an argument that anything goes as long as it's called "trading." The whole case I've made rests on quant finance operating inside a framework of fair rules, and that framework matters because public markets serve a different social function than private ones.

Public markets exist, in part, as a shared utility: the prices they produce get used by everyone, from a pension fund deciding how to invest, to a company deciding whether to raise capital, to an ordinary person checking their retirement account. Because that price signal is public infrastructure in a very real sense, manipulating it doesn't just hurt one counterparty, it degrades a resource the entire economy relies on. That's why public markets need strict, actively enforced rules around fair dealing, and why manipulation there is a much bigger deal than a bad private deal between two consenting parties.

Private markets, like private equity or bilateral OTC deals, are a different story. They still serve a real function, mainly capital formation for entities that don't want or qualify for public market scrutiny, but the case for their societal necessity is weaker, since their pricing and outcomes mostly stay contained to the parties involved rather than feeding a public signal everyone relies on. I'd still call them legitimate, just on a shakier moral footing than the public price discovery argument I've been making for market making and HFT.

## A Case Study in Where the Line Gets Crossed: SEBI vs. Jane Street

I think the clearest illustration of this whole argument, that quant finance is moral specifically because it operates within fair rules, and immoral the moment it doesn't, is the ongoing case between India's securities regulator, SEBI, and the American proprietary trading firm Jane Street.

In July 2025, SEBI issued an interim order accusing Jane Street of manipulating the Bank Nifty index on multiple expiry days between 2023 and 2025. According to SEBI's findings, the firm allegedly bought large volumes of Bank Nifty stocks and futures early in the trading day to push the index up, then reversed those positions and sold aggressively by the close, profiting from options positions that depended on exactly that kind of swing. SEBI impounded roughly ₹4,843 crore (about $565 million) in what it called unlawful gains and temporarily barred the firm from Indian markets. Jane Street paid the amount into escrow and resumed trading about two weeks later, and has since appealed the order, with the underlying investigation and legal fight still ongoing as of this writing.

I bring this up not to pass judgment on Jane Street specifically, since the legal process is still playing out, but because the alleged conduct is a near perfect negative image of everything I argued above. Legitimate market making pushes prices toward their true value and profits from providing liquidity at a fair edge. What SEBI alleges here is closer to the opposite: deliberately pushing a price away from its true value, then profiting from a derivatives position that depended on that distortion, at the expense of the retail traders on the other side who had no idea the index they were trading was being nudged around them. If the allegations hold up, this isn't price discovery accelerated by speed, it's the appearance of price discovery being manufactured for private gain. That's exactly the distinction I mean when I keep saying quant finance is moral "under the constraints of standard law." The tools (speed, capital, statistical modeling) are the same tools that make legitimate market making valuable. What determines whether their use is moral is whether the resulting price reflects reality or distorts it, and whether the profit comes from serving the market or from exploiting it.

## One More Objection: Isn't This Just Extraction From Retail Investors?

The strongest version of the critique I haven't addressed yet is something like: even granting that market making and price discovery are valuable, does society really need to pay quants what it does for it, and doesn't that money ultimately come out of retail investors' returns? I don't think this holds up as well as it first sounds, though it deserves a fair answer rather than a dismissal.

Most retail investors aren't actively trading against quants in the way this objection assumes. The typical retail investor is buying and holding a broad index fund, something like VOO, in a retirement account, and rebalancing rarely if at all. That's a voluntary decision, and it doesn't require them to trade frequently or accept unfavorable fills from anyone. I'll admit even a passive investor isn't entirely insulated: every time a fund creates or redeems shares, or rebalances, it interacts with market makers, so a small bid-ask cost does pass through eventually. But that cost is exactly the kind of thing competition keeps thin, for the same reason I described in the market making section: multiple firms competing for that order flow squeeze the edge toward the actual cost of the service rather than letting any one of them charge whatever they want. Where quants profit more meaningfully from trading against someone, it's typically other institutions or active participants who chose to transact frequently, a voluntary exchange between willing counterparties, not an involuntary tax on someone's retirement savings.

The size of the salaries isn't independent evidence of extraction either, though it's worth being accurate about what those salaries actually look like. The eye-catching eight-figure numbers people cite belong to a small tail of senior portfolio managers and partners, not the profession as a whole; most quants earn well, but nowhere near that. Whatever the number, quant compensation is set by the same supply and demand that prices every other scarce skill in the economy. The market pays a premium for the combination of statistics, engineering, and risk management this work requires, the same way it pays a premium for scarce skill sets elsewhere. A high salary reflects how few people can do the job well, not proof that the job is siphoning value from somewhere else.

## Where This Leaves Us

I don't think quantitative finance is a morally neutral career that happens to pay well, and I don't think it's a parasitic one either. The core functions, price discovery and market making, are genuinely necessary for a market economy to allocate resources well and for ordinary people to trust participating in it at all. Speed and automation, embodied in HFT, extend those same functions rather than replacing them with something more dubious. What makes the whole enterprise moral is that it operates inside a set of rules designed to keep prices honest, and the moment someone steps outside those rules, as SEBI alleges happened here, the same tools stop serving the public function I've been describing and start undermining it.

So I'd push back on the instinct to judge this career by its paycheck or its opacity, and judge it instead by what the work actually does when it's done within the rules. Go back to the doctor from the start of this piece. Nobody thinks medicine is immoral because it's lucrative or because most people don't understand how a kidney transplant works. We judge it by whether the doctor is healing people within the rules that govern the profession, and we'd revoke that moral standing instantly the moment they stepped outside those rules. I'm just asking that quant finance get judged by the same standard, rather than a harsher one reserved for careers that happen to look opaque and pay well. By that standard, I think it holds up better than its reputation suggests.
